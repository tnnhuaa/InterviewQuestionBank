## Slide 011 — 2. Create a Product Roadmap

_PDF page 2, handout panel 5_

```text
2. Create a Product Roadmap
•
Product roadmap: A series of releases for achieving some or all of
our product vision.
•
If we are planning only a single small release, we don’t need a
product roadmap.
•
Releasing frequently doesn’t mean we set overly aggressive
deadlines; such deadlines frequently result in missed dates.
•
Instead, we focus each release on a small set of minimum
releasable features (MRFs) around which the stakeholder
community shares a strong group consensus.
```

## Slide 012 — Product Roadmap Duration

_PDF page 2, handout panel 6_

```text
Product Roadmap Duration
•
When using Scrum, we produce the product roadmap as far into the
future as is reasonable and desirable.
•
How far into the future your roadmap should extend will depend on
your particular circumstances.
•
At a minimum, your roadmap will probably need to cover at least
the span of time you are asking people to fund.
```

_PDF page 3, handout panel 2_

```text
Release Planning [3]
•
Release planning is longer-term planning that enables us to answer
questions like “When will we be done?” or “Which features can I get by
the end of the year?” or “How much will this cost?”

Release planning is not a one-time event but rather a frequent, every-
sprint activity.

features (MRFs).
```

## Slide 021 — 7. Create a Fixed-Date Release Plan (I)

_PDF page 4, handout panel 3_

```text
7. Create a Fixed-Date Release Plan (I)
• Determine how many sprints are in this release.
• Groom the product backlog to a sufficient depth by creating,
estimating the size of, and prioritizing product backlog items.
• Measure or estimate the team’s velocity as a range (i.e.
determine an average faster and an average slower velocity
for the team).
```

## Slide 022 — 7. Create a Fixed-Date Release Plan (II)

_PDF page 4, handout panel 4_

```text
7. Create a Fixed-Date Release Plan (II)
• Multiply the slower velocity by the
number of sprints.
• Count down that number of points
into the product backlog and draw a

• Multiply the faster velocity by the

second line. This is the “might-have”
line.
```

## Slide 026 — 7. Create a Fixed-Scope Release Plan (II)

_PDF page 5, handout panel 2_

```text
7. Create a Fixed-Scope Release Plan (II)
• Divide the total size of the PBIs by the faster velocity and
round up the answer to the next integer. This will tell us the
lowest number of sprints required to deliver the features.

highest number of sprints required to deliver the features.
```

## Slide 028 — 8. Calculate Release Cost

_PDF page 5, handout panel 4_

```text
8. Calculate Release Cost
• Determine who is on the team.
• Determine the sprint length.
• Based on team composition and sprint length,
determine the personnel costs of running a
sprint.
• For a fixed-date release, multiply the number
of sprints in the release by the cost per sprint.
• For a fixed-scope release, multiply both the
high and low number of sprints by the cost per
sprint. The results are the range of personnel
costs associated with the release.
```

## Slide 033 — How Should Architectural Design Be Done?

_PDF page 6, handout panel 3_

```text
How Should Architectural Design Be Done?
• Often the architect identifies the major components then
defines the interfaces between them (including nonfunctional
requirements like security, speed and reliability) and
delegates the internal design of the components to individual
teams.
• An overview consisting of a high level network or component
diagram and a page of text. This is for a very wide audience,
from upper management down to dev and QA. This rarely
uses UML or a defined notation due to the wide audience.
• In agile environments, less of the design is done upfront and
more of it is delegated to component teams.
```

## Slide 051 — Agile Fixed-Price Contract Review

_PDF page 9, handout panel 3_

```text
Agile Fixed-Price Contract Review
•
An agile fixed price contract is similar to a time and material contract, in which, a
client is charged by time and materials and any party can leave the contract with a
reasonable lead time.
•
The 4 key differences are that:
–
A rough estimate (which is a “fixed” price) is made based on a rough initial product
backlog.
–
If the actual cost exceeds the rough estimate then both client and supplier share the
cost (the time and materials cost then is only 30% or 40% of the original charge. This
value is negotiated.)
–
Requirements can be changed and will be evaluated against the “fixed” price by a board
and negotiated within the “fixed” price. Usually, this will replace an old backlog item
with a new one (for supplier’s safety).
–
Quality working software with adequate documentation is delivered every 2-3 weeks to
client (for client’s safety).
•
IMO, I think the weakest point is that a supplier can leave the contract as soon as
all the budget (“fixed” price) is spent despite getting a bad reputation.
```
